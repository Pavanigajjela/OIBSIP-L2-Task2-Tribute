Marie Curie

The first woman to win a Nobel Prize and the only person to win in two different scientific fields

Marie Curie (c. 1920)

Biography

Marie Curie, born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw, Poland in 1867, was a physicist and chemist who conducted pioneering research on radioactivity. Despite facing significant barriers as a woman in science during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, she persevered through adversity to become one of the most influential scientists in history. Her journey began at the Flying University, an underground educational enterprise in Warsaw that admitted women, before she moved to Paris to continue her studies at the Sorbonne.

In Paris, Marie met Pierre Curie, a professor of physics, and they married in 1895. Their partnership would prove to be one of the most collaborative and productive in scientific history. Together, they worked in a converted dissection shed behind the School of Physics and Chemistry in Paris, where they conducted groundbreaking research on radioactive materials. Their dedication was so intense that they often forgot to eat or sleep while processing tons of pitchblende ore to isolate tiny amounts of new elements.

Marie Curie's most significant contributions include the discovery of two new elements: polonium, named after her native Poland, and radium. She developed techniques for isolating radioactive isotopes and conducted extensive research on their medical applications, particularly in the treatment of cancer. Her work laid the foundation for modern nuclear physics and medical treatments that continue to save countless lives today. She also coined the term "radioactivity" itself, providing a fundamental concept for understanding atomic structure.

Tragedy struck in 1906 when Pierre was killed in a street accident, but Marie continued their research with remarkable resilience. She took over his teaching position at the Sorbonne, becoming the university's first female professor. During World War I, she developed mobile radiography units known as "Little Curies" to provide X-ray services to field hospitals, personally driving them to the front lines. Her lifelong exposure to radiation eventually took its toll, and she died in 1934 from aplastic anemia, likely caused by her research.

Key Achievements

1891

Studies at the Sorbonne

Moved to Paris to study physics and mathematics at the University of Paris, earning degrees in both subjects.

1895

Marriage to Pierre Curie

Married Pierre Curie, beginning their legendary scientific partnership.

1898

Discovery of Polonium and Radium

Discovered two new radioactive elements, naming polonium after her homeland and radium for its intense radioactivity.

1903

First Nobel Prize

Awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics (shared with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel) for their work on radioactivity phenomena.

1911

Second Nobel Prize

Became the first person to win two Nobel Prizes when she received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of radium and polonium.

1914-1918

World War I Service

Developed mobile X-ray units and trained women to operate them, providing critical medical services to wounded soldiers.
